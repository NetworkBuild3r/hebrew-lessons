# Prior-art review — How adults best learn a new language in self-study (RSCH-003)

**Research type:** `prior_art_review` \
**Subject:** Evidence on how adults (self-study, any target language) best acquire a second or foreign language: effective practices and ineffective or overrated practices \
**Date:** 2026-10-08 \
**Requestor:** session user \
**Identifier:** `RSCH-003` \
**Access scope:** `NARROW` (public sources only; see Appendix A)

---

## 1. Findings

**Recommendation:** An adult self-study program built on published evidence should do five things. It should bring every item back for recall from memory on later days, not in one sitting [1][2]. It should supply large amounts of reading and listening the learner can mostly understand, at roughly 95–98% known words; that threshold comes from reading research [3], and applying it to listening is an extrapolation. It should add short, focused grammar or pattern work with feedback that prompts the learner to correct the error [4][5][6]. It should train listening perception of difficult sounds [7][8] and pronunciation with feedback [9], both at Moderate confidence. It should keep a steady daily habit tied to a fixed cue [10][11], though this rests on one general habit study and a summary of it (Low). It should drop rereading, highlighting, and learning-style matching, which the evidence does not support [12][13]. The memory findings are the strongest part of this evidence. The grammar findings are the most contested, and the evidence on apps, session length, total practice time, and habit formation is the thinnest. See also RSCH-001 (cross-reference, not evidence); this report rests on its own sources. **Moderate**

### 1.1 Memory: spacing, retrieval, flashcards, recall direction, recall versus recognition, interleaving, encounters

- Spacing out practice beats massing it, in general memory research and in second-language research specifically. A meta-analysis of 29 studies found a strong benefit of spaced over massed retrieval practice (g = 0.74) [1]. A second-language meta-analysis of 48 experiments (N = 3,411) found a medium-to-large spacing effect [2]. A major review of ten study techniques rated distributed practice as high utility [12]. **High: three independent sources agree. General learning science and second-language research.**
- Recalling an item from memory works better than reading it again. The spacing meta-analysis reports two earlier meta-analyses of retrieval practice: g = 0.50 against restudy, and g = 0.61 against all other learning activities [1]. The study-techniques review rated practice testing as high utility and rereading as low utility, and advised using other techniques in place of rereading [12]. **Moderate: one high-quality review read directly [12]; [1] reports the earlier retrieval meta-analyses secondhand. General learning science applied to languages.**
- Steadily expanding review gaps are not better than equal gaps. Expanding and uniform schedules did not differ in the general meta-analysis (g = 0.034), although expanding schedules did better when items were tested more times [1]. In second-language studies, equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent, and longer gaps beat shorter gaps on delayed tests, though not on immediate ones [2]. **Moderate for "equal is as good as expanding": two sources agree. Moderate for "longer gaps help long-term retention": one second-language meta-analysis. General learning science and second-language research.**
- Word-focused activities such as flashcards and word lists produce real gains, but much is forgotten after a single study session. Across 22 studies of flashcards, word lists, writing, and fill-in-the-blank tasks, immediate gains averaged 60.1% (meaning recall) and 58.5% (form recall), and fell to 39.4% and 25.1% on delayed tests. Gains ranged from 18.4% to 77.0% depending on the activity, and the authors conclude that learning through word-focused tasks is far from guaranteed [14]. **Moderate: one meta-analysis. Second-language research.**
- The direction of recall matters. Recalling the meaning from the target word (receptive recall) builds mainly receptive knowledge, and recalling the target word from its meaning (productive recall) builds productive knowledge, so flashcard software should support both [15]. In a word-pair study, productive learning produced larger gains across more aspects of word knowledge, and the author concluded that productive learning may be the better single choice [16]. **Moderate: two sources agree, although [15]'s recommendation rests partly on [16], so the two are not fully independent. Second-language research.**
- Recall formats (typing or saying the answer from memory) may help more than recognition formats (choosing among options, as in multiple choice), but the evidence conflicts. A meta-analysis of testing-effect studies found that initial recall tests produced larger benefits than recognition tests, which the author took as support for effortful processing [17]. An open-access study of testing in university teaching characterizes that meta-analysis as resting on laboratory research. It reports that a later meta-analysis including applied classroom studies (Adesope and colleagues, 2017) found the opposite, with multiple-choice practice tests stronger than short-answer tests, and suggests the difference may reflect applied versus laboratory settings [18]. In its own university-course experiment, the same study found a testing effect for short-answer questions only, which favors recall [18]. For second-language vocabulary, a flashcard-software review applies the retrieval-effort hypothesis and recommends starting with easier recognition formats and moving to harder recall formats, but it is a design review, not a comparison study [15]. No direct second-language comparison of recall versus recognition practice was found, so this remains an open question. **Low: two meta-analyses conflict, one of them seen only through a secondary report, and no direct second-language test was found. The evidence leans toward recall, because [18]'s own experiment found a testing effect for short-answer questions only. General learning science, with one second-language design review.**
- There is no single number of encounters that guarantees a word is learned. Across 26 studies (N = 1,918), the number of encounters correlated with incidental word learning at r = .34, a medium effect, and spacing, engagement, and visual support changed the size of that effect [19]. Learners picked up only 9–18% of target words from meaning-focused input on immediate tests [20]. **Moderate that more encounters help: one meta-analysis, consistent with a second. Low for "no fixed threshold exists": an absence claim.**
- Mixing different patterns in one practice set (interleaving) helps longer-term retention. The study-techniques review rated interleaving as moderate utility, because the evidence was still limited [12]. In a second-language grammar experiment with 115 learners, interleaved practice produced more errors during training but beat blocked practice on a one-week delayed test, and learners with lower prior knowledge benefited more [21]. **Moderate: two sources agree, one of them a single study. General learning science with one second-language experiment.**

### 1.2 Input: comprehensible input, extensive reading and listening, coverage, glosses, reading while listening

- Reading becomes comfortable only when nearly all words are known. A study of learners' vocabulary size, text coverage, and reading scores proposed two thresholds: a minimal one of about 95% text coverage (4,000–5,000 word families) and an optimal one of about 98% (about 8,000 word families), both counting proper nouns. The authors discuss these figures alongside earlier work by Hu and Nation (2000) and Nation (2006), which was not read here [3]. These thresholds come from reading comprehension. No listening-specific coverage study was read in this session, so applying the 95–98% range to listening is an extrapolation from reading and is listed as an open question. **Low for the reading thresholds: one primary study read. Very Low for applying them to listening: an inference. Second-language research.**
- Extensive reading improves reading proficiency, with small-to-medium effects. A meta-analysis of 49 studies (5,919 participants) found small-to-medium effects in both study designs, and a higher effect for adults than for children and adolescents [22]. **Moderate: one meta-analysis. Second-language research.**
- Glosses (short meaning notes on unknown words) raise word learning from reading substantially, and first-language glosses beat target-language glosses. Across 42 studies (N = 3,802), glossed reading led to 45.3% (immediate) and 33.4% (delayed) learning of target words, against 26.6% and 19.8% without glosses. Multiple-choice glosses were most effective, and in-text glosses and end glossaries least effective. First-language glosses gave greater learning than target-language glosses, and the authors found no interaction between gloss language and learner proficiency [23]. A separate meta-analysis of 26 studies found first-language glosses more effective than target-language glosses (g = 0.33), and reported the advantage as especially strong for beginners [24]. **Moderate that first-language glosses beat target-language glosses: two independent meta-analyses agree. Low that the advantage is specific to beginners: only [24] supports it, and [23] found no interaction with proficiency. Second-language research.**
- Picking up words from input alone is real but slow, so deliberate study should run alongside it. Meaning-focused input produced large effects but only 9–18% of target words learned per study on immediate tests [20], while deliberate word-focused activities averaged about 60% immediately [14]. **Moderate for each figure separately. Low for the direct comparison, because the two meta-analyses come from the same research group and use different designs. Second-language research.**
- Reading while listening is not reliably better than reading alone. A meta-analysis of 30 studies found only a trivial overall comprehension benefit (g = 0.18). The benefit appeared when the reading-only condition was paced by the experimenter (g = 0.41) and not when learners paced their own reading (g = 0.06) [25]. For incidental word learning, reading, listening, and reading while listening gave similar gains, while viewing gave smaller gains (7% immediate) [20]. **Moderate: two sources agree that reading while listening is not clearly superior. Second-language research.**

### 1.3 Grammar, corrective feedback, speaking, pronunciation, shadowing, perception training

- Focused instruction on language forms helps second-language learners. A meta-analysis of 54 studies (5,051 learners) found a large overall effect of second-language instruction (g = 1.06) [5]. A meta-analysis of 41 studies found that explicit instruction helped both controlled knowledge and spontaneous use of simple and complex grammar features [4]. **Moderate: two meta-analyses agree that instruction helps. Second-language research.**
- Whether explicit rule teaching beats implicit exposure is unresolved. One meta-analysis found larger effects for explicit than implicit instruction on both simple and complex features [4]. A later meta-analysis found only a minor difference between them overall [5]. **Low: unresolved conflict between sources. Second-language research.**
- Corrective feedback works. Prompts that push the learner to self-correct did at least as well as recasts (where the correct form is simply restated). In 15 classroom studies (N = 827), oral feedback had significant and durable effects (d = 0.74 between groups). Recasts reached d = 0.53, prompts d = 0.83, and explicit correction d = 0.84, and prompts beat recasts in within-group contrasts [6]. A second meta-analysis of 33 studies found a medium overall effect that was maintained over time, with implicit feedback better maintained than explicit feedback [26]. **Moderate that feedback works: two sources agree. Low for which feedback type is best: the two sources point in different directions on explicit versus implicit feedback. Second-language research.**
- Interaction and speaking practice support development, but this session reached that evidence only through a secondary summary. A dissertation meta-analysis reports that task-based interaction is associated with medium and large effects, in line with earlier meta-analyses including Mackey and Goo (2007) [27]. The specific Mackey and Goo effect sizes could not be confirmed in the public text, so none are given here; reading that meta-analysis directly is an open question. **Low: one secondary report of a meta-analysis that was not read directly. Second-language research.**
- Pronunciation instruction works, especially when it includes feedback and runs longer. Across 86 reports, pronunciation instruction produced large effects (d = 0.89 within groups and d = 0.80 between groups). Effects were larger for longer interventions, for treatments with feedback, and for controlled outcome measures [9]. **Moderate: one large meta-analysis, consistent with the shadowing review below. Second-language research.**
- Perception training with immediate feedback (high-variability phonetic training) improves listening for difficult sounds, and the gains are retained. A meta-analysis of 79 studies found medium-to-large perception effects (g = 0.92 pre to post; g = 0.67 against controls), confirmed long-term retention, and found some generalization to new items [7]. A synthesis of 32 studies called the technique very effective, with long-lasting improvement [8], but its author (Ron Thomson) is also an author of [7], so it is not independent support. Whether the "many different voices" part specifically adds benefit is unsettled, and the evidence points both ways. A meta-analysis that compared multi-talker with single-talker training found large multi-talker advantages for generalization to new talkers (g = 0.72) and for long-term retention (g = 1.09) [28]. In the same meta-analysis, the multi-talker advantage on immediate outcomes fell from g = 0.46 to about g = −0.04 after outliers were removed and publication bias corrected [28]. [7] also cites contrary evidence on the multi-talker benefit (Brekelmans and colleagues, 2022, not read here). **Moderate that perception training improves perception and the gains are retained: one large, high-quality meta-analysis [7], with a supporting synthesis [8] that shares an author with it and is not counted as independent. Low that many different voices specifically add benefit: the sources conflict. Second-language research.**
- Perception gains may carry over to speaking only partly, but this session reached that evidence only secondhand. [7] reports its authors' 2024 meta-analysis of the same training's effects on production, which found small-to-medium production gains (g = .49 to .66) but weak support for retention or generalization. That production meta-analysis was not read directly. **Low: one secondhand report of a meta-analysis that was not read directly. Second-language research.**
- Shadowing (repeating audio aloud almost at the same time as hearing it) may help comprehensibility, fluency, and rhythm, but the evidence is weak. A systematic review of 44 studies found improvements in comprehensibility, intelligibility, accentedness, fluency, and some prosody. Results for individual sounds were inconclusive, and the review notes heavy reliance on controlled tasks [29]. **Low: one narrative review with methodological caveats. Second-language research.**

### 1.4 What does not work, or is overrated

- Rereading and highlighting are low-value study methods. They were rated low utility because they do not consistently improve performance [12]. Retrieval beat restudy, and other learning activities, in the meta-analyses reported in [1]. **Moderate: two sources agree. General learning science applied to languages.**
- Matching instruction to a "learning style" is not supported. A major review found virtually no studies showing the required crossover pattern, and several appropriate studies contradicted it [13]. The disconfirmation search found a 2024 meta-analysis with a small overall matching benefit (g = 0.31). However, only 26% of outcome measures showed the crossover pattern, and the authors judged the benefit too small and infrequent to justify adoption [30]. In a second-language vocabulary study (N = 128), mixed-modality presentation gave the best learning and retention. Matching did beat mismatching on retention, and learners of all styles were equally successful [31]. **Moderate: three sources agree that matching should not drive design, with one partial counter-signal. General learning science, with one second-language study.**
- Cramming many repetitions into one sitting is the weaker schedule. This follows directly from the spacing findings above [1][2][12]. **High. General learning science and second-language research.**
- "Adults can't really learn a new language" is a myth, and so is "age doesn't matter." In a dataset of 669,498 speakers, grammar-learning ability was preserved until about age 17.4 and then declined steadily. The authors note that people who start in adulthood often keep an accent and conspicuous grammatical errors. The same article notes that in early stages adults perform better than children, perhaps because they use conscious strategies and their first language [32]. **Moderate for the decline in native-like grammar attainment: one very large study. Low for "adults learn faster early on": reported in [32] from earlier studies not read here. Second-language research.**
- Language apps and gamification have little independent efficacy evidence. A systematic review of 35 Duolingo studies (2012–2020) found mostly design-focused, quantitative work with non-probability sampling, and more focus on building tools than on learning outcomes [33]. An independent semester-long study with nine learners studying Turkish found improvement and a moderate correlation between time spent and gains. It also notes that the favorable early study was commissioned by the company, and that learners reported variable motivation and frustration with the materials [34]. **Low: thin and mostly weak evidence, and an absence of strong independent trials. Second-language research.**
- Sleep is not a substitute for study. Playing previously learned foreign words during non-REM sleep improved later recall of their translations in a lab study, which strengthens words already studied while awake [35]. New word pairs played during deep sleep left only weak implicit associations. Overall accuracy on a later forced-choice test was about 52.7%, and performance reached about 10% above chance only when the second word repeatedly (in 2–4 of its 4 presentations) landed on a slow-wave peak; a single landing left performance at chance [36]. At home over three nights, sleep cueing showed no overall benefit, and a benefit appeared only on the third night or in people without sleep disturbances [37]. Subliminal learning was not researched in this session. **Low: lab-bound and implicit effects, and no evidence of usable vocabulary learned from scratch during sleep (an absence claim). General memory science applied to languages.**
- Banning the first language removes a support that helps learners. First-language glosses beat target-language glosses (g = 0.33) in one meta-analysis [24] and gave greater learning in a second [23]. The first reports the advantage as especially strong for beginners [24], but the second found no interaction between gloss language and proficiency [23]. **Moderate for first-language glosses over target-language glosses. Low for a beginner-specific advantage: the two sources conflict. The broader classroom question of how much first language to use was not covered. Second-language research.**

### 1.5 Motivation, habit, and time

- Reaching professional-level proficiency takes hundreds to thousands of hours, depending on the language. The U.S. State Department's Foreign Service Institute estimates the time to reach an integrated score of 3 (Speaking + Listening) on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale. Its estimates are 24–30 weeks (552–690 class hours) for languages close to English, about 44 weeks (1,012 class hours) for "hard" languages including Hebrew, and 88 weeks (2,200 class hours) for Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, and Korean. A typical week is 23 class hours plus 17 hours of self-study, and the page says the time varies with aptitude, prior language experience, and classroom time [38]. **Moderate as a statement of the published estimates: one first-party source quoted directly, current as of retrieval. Very Low as a forecast for a self-study adult: these figures come from full-time, instructor-led training aimed at a high level, so applying them to self-study is an inference.**
- Whether more total practice time produces proportionally more learning is not settled by this evidence. Time spent on the app correlated moderately with gains in the semester-long Duolingo study, but that study was correlational and had only nine learners [34]. Longer pronunciation interventions produced larger effects [9]. Against this, a corrective-feedback meta-analysis found that shorter treatments produced larger effects than longer ones [26]. The oral-feedback meta-analysis reported larger effects for long than for short-to-medium treatments, but could not distinguish brief treatments from long ones [6]. The second-language spacing meta-analysis lists the number of sessions as a moderator of spacing effects, but its abstract does not say in which direction [2]. **Low: the sources conflict, and the supporting evidence is correlational or indirect. Second-language research.**
- No direct evidence on the best length for a single session was found. The spacing evidence favors spreading the same practice over more sessions [1][2]. **Low: an absence of direct evidence, plus an application of the spacing results.**
- Motivation predicts what learners say they will do much better than what they achieve. In a meta-analysis of 32 reports (32,078 learners), the "ideal second-language self" correlated .61 with intended effort but only .20 with measured achievement. After correction for publication bias, the achievement correlation was .10 and no longer significant [39]. **Moderate: one meta-analysis. Second-language research.**
- Habits form through repetition in a stable daily context, and missing one opportunity did not materially affect the habit-formation process in the one study read. Ninety-six volunteers repeated a daily behavior after a fixed cue. Time to reach 95% of peak automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days, more consistent performance was associated with a better fit of the habit-formation curve, and missing one opportunity to perform the behavior did not materially affect the habit-formation process [10]. A summary of the same study reports an average of 66 days, and some evidence that many missed days, even spread out, lowered the final level of automaticity [11]. **Low: one primary study and a secondary summary of it. General behavior science applied to languages.**
- Self-efficacy was not covered. No source on self-efficacy was retrieved within budget, so this remains an open question.

### Do

- **Schedule delayed recall of every item across separate days, not repeated in one sitting.** Spaced practice beats massed practice in general and in second-language research [1][2][12]. **High · general learning science and second-language research.**
- **Make the learner recall the answer before showing it, with feedback, instead of letting them reread.** Retrieval beats restudy [1][12]. **Moderate · general learning science applied to languages.**
- **Use simple review intervals and lengthen them for long-term goals.** Equal intervals were as effective as expanding ones [1][2], and longer gaps helped delayed retention [2]. **Moderate · general learning science and second-language research.**
- **Practice both recall directions, and include target-word production if speaking or writing is the goal.** Each direction builds its own kind of knowledge [15][16]. **Moderate · second-language research.**
- **Include recall from memory (typing or saying the answer), not only multiple choice, and consider moving new items from recognition to recall as they become familiar.** Recall tests gave larger benefits than recognition tests in a meta-analysis [17] that [18] characterizes as laboratory-based, and [18]'s own university-course experiment found a testing effect for short-answer questions only. Against this, a meta-analysis that included classroom studies found multiple-choice practice stronger than short-answer practice, as reported in [18]. A flashcard-software review recommends ordering formats from easier recognition to harder recall [15]. **Low, leaning toward recall · general learning science, with one second-language design review.**
- **Bring each word back many times, in different contexts.** More encounters predict more learning (r = .34), and one session yields far less on delayed tests [14][19]. **Moderate · second-language research.**
- **Mix different patterns and item types within a review set once they have been introduced.** Interleaving improved delayed retention [12][21]. **Moderate · general learning science with one second-language experiment.**
- **Give a large volume of reading at about 95% known words or higher, aiming for 98% as the optimal level, and apply the same target to listening as a provisional rule.** The coverage thresholds come from reading research [3]; the listening application is extrapolated from reading. Extensive reading benefits come from [22]. **Low for the reading thresholds, Very Low for applying them to listening, Moderate for extensive reading · second-language research.**
- **Offer glosses on unknown words, in the first language by default, and consider multiple-choice glosses.** Glosses raised word learning, and first-language glosses beat target-language glosses [23][24]. Whether this matters more for beginners is unsettled: one meta-analysis reports it does [24], and the other found no interaction with proficiency [23]. **Moderate for glosses and first-language glosses, Low for the beginner-specific part · second-language research.**
- **Pair input with deliberate word study, because input alone adds words slowly.** Compare 9–18% per study from input [20] with about 60% immediate from word-focused tasks [14]. **Low for the comparison · second-language research.**
- **Include short, focused grammar or pattern instruction followed by meaningful practice.** Instruction helps [4][5]. **Moderate · second-language research.**
- **Give corrective feedback, and where possible prompt the learner to try again before showing the answer.** Feedback is effective and durable [6][26]. Prompts did at least as well as recasts [6]. **Moderate for feedback, Low for prompts over recasts · second-language research.**
- **Train listening perception of difficult sounds with immediate feedback, using several voices where available.** Perception gains were medium-to-large and retained in one large meta-analysis [7], with a supporting synthesis by one of its authors [8]. Whether many different voices add benefit is unsettled. Multi-talker training showed large advantages over single-talker training for generalization and retention, but its immediate advantage vanished after bias correction [28], and [7] cites a contrary study. **Moderate for perception training, Low for the many-voices part · second-language research.**
- **Train pronunciation with feedback over weeks, and consider pairing perception drills with speaking practice on the same sounds.** Feedback and longer interventions gave larger pronunciation effects [9]. The pairing is a reasonable precaution rather than a tested design: the only evidence that perception gains carry over to speech only partly is [7]'s secondhand report of a production meta-analysis not read here. **Moderate for pronunciation with feedback, Low for the perception-to-speaking part · second-language research.**
- **Include speaking aimed at a real message, not only drills.** Interaction was associated with gains in grammar and vocabulary [27]. **Low · second-language research.**
- **Tie daily study to a fixed cue, and don't treat a single missed day as a failure.** Consistent repetition in a stable context builds automaticity, and missing one opportunity did not materially affect the habit-formation process [10], though a summary of the same study reports that many missed days lowered the final level of automaticity [11]. **Low · general behavior science applied to languages.**
- **Set expectations in hundreds of hours and track total time.** The published training estimates support the scale [38]. Whether more total time produces proportionally more learning is unsettled, because the time-on-task sources conflict [2][6][9][26][34]. **Moderate for the published figures, Very Low as a self-study forecast, Low for the time-on-task link · second-language program data and research.**

### Don't

- **Don't rely on rereading or highlighting as review.** Both were rated low utility [12], and retrieval beats rereading [1]. **Moderate · general learning science applied to languages.**
- **Don't cram reviews into one long sitting.** Massed practice loses to spaced practice [1][2][12]. **High · general learning science and second-language research.**
- **Don't build a "learning style" quiz or tailor content to it. Present words and patterns in several modalities to everyone.** Matching lacks crossover evidence [13][30], and mixed modality did best in a second-language study [31]. **Moderate · general learning science, with one second-language study.**
- **Don't tell adults they are too old to learn, but don't promise native-like grammar either.** Grammar-learning ability declines after late adolescence, yet adults do well in early stages [32]. **Moderate for the decline, Low for the early-stage advantage · second-language research.**
- **Don't treat a gamified app, streaks, or points as proven on their own.** Independent efficacy evidence is thin and mostly weak [33][34]. **Low · second-language research.**
- **Don't count on sleep audio or subliminal exposure to teach new material.** In the lab, sleep cueing strengthened words already studied while awake [35], and new words played during sleep left only weak implicit traces [36]. At home, cueing showed no overall benefit [37]. Subliminal learning was not researched here. **Low · general memory science applied to languages.**
- **Don't ban first-language translation support.** First-language glosses beat target-language glosses [23][24]. One meta-analysis reports the advantage as strongest for beginners [24], but another found no interaction with proficiency [23]. **Moderate for first-language glosses, Low for the beginner-specific part · second-language research.**
- **Don't force synchronized audio on every text as if it always helps.** Reading while listening gave a trivial comprehension benefit when learners controlled the pace [25], and similar word gains to reading alone [20]. **Moderate · second-language research.**
- **Don't rely on motivation or vision-setting to carry achievement.** Motivation correlated strongly with intended effort but weakly with measured achievement [39]. **Moderate · second-language research.**
- **Don't commit to one grammar philosophy, whether all rules or no rules.** The explicit-versus-implicit evidence conflicts [4][5]. **Low · second-language research.**
- **Don't assume that training learners to hear a sound will, on its own, fix how they say it.** A production meta-analysis reported secondhand in [7], and not read here, found smaller gains in production than [7] found for perception, with weak support for their retention. **Low · second-language research.**

## 2. Confidence

**Overall confidence:** Moderate

**Justification:** The memory findings are strong. Spacing rests on three independent sources, including two meta-analyses, one of them specific to second-language learning, and a major review [1][2][12]. Perception training rests on one large meta-analysis [7] plus a synthesis [8] by a co-author of [7], which is not independent, so perception gains are Moderate under the one-high-quality-source clause. The claim that many different voices specifically add benefit conflicts across sources and is Low [7][28]. Transfer from perception training to speaking is Low, because it rests only on [7]'s secondhand report of a production meta-analysis that was not read directly. Most other findings rest on one or two meta-analyses each, so they sit at Moderate. The grammar question (explicit versus implicit) has an unresolved conflict between meta-analyses and is held at Low [4][5]. Three more questions have conflicting sources and are held at Low: recall versus recognition formats, where the evidence leans toward recall [17][18], whether more total practice time produces more learning [2][6][9][26][34], and whether first-language glosses help beginners in particular [23][24]. App efficacy, sleep learning, interaction, shadowing, habit formation, and session length rest on thin, secondary, or absent evidence and are Low. Applying the reading coverage thresholds to listening is an inference and is Very Low. Several sources come from one research group (Webb, Uchihara, Yanagisawa, and colleagues), which limits independence for some vocabulary claims [14][19][20][23]. Where only a publisher's public abstract was available, only the abstract was used. The investigation used 30 web calls, the hard stop. That is above the prescribed ~15–20 because five sub-questions each needed retrieval plus a disconfirmation search. Synthesis was not forced early by the budget, but several sub-questions have named residuals, listed below. The first remediation loop used 10 more web calls to repair citations and add the recall-versus-recognition finding. It lowered several bands and raised none, so the overall band stays Moderate. The second remediation loop used no web calls. It stopped citing the talker-variability meta-analysis [28] for perception training in general or for transfer to speaking, lowered the transfer finding to Low, and made minor accuracy fixes. It lowered bands and raised none. The overall band stays Moderate, because the central recommendations still rest on High or Moderate findings.

**Coverage, scored per sub-question and not averaged:**

- **Memory (spacing, retrieval, flashcards, recall direction, recall versus recognition, interleaving, encounters): about 0.85, answered with a residual.** Several independent meta-analyses support spacing and retrieval. The disconfirmation found that expanding schedules are not superior, which revises a common belief rather than overturning spacing. Recall versus recognition is Low: two general meta-analyses conflict, and no direct second-language comparison was found, so it stays an open question, although [18]'s own experiment leans toward recall.
- **Input (coverage, extensive reading and listening, glosses, incidental versus deliberate, reading while listening): about 0.75, answered with a residual.** The coverage thresholds rest on one reading study read directly. No listening-specific coverage study was found, so the listening threshold is extrapolated from reading and stays an open question. Several sources share one research group.
- **Grammar, feedback, speaking, pronunciation, perception: about 0.75, answered with a residual.** The explicit-versus-implicit conflict is unresolved. Interaction evidence was reached only through a secondary summary. The many-voices component of perception training is Low because the sources conflict. Transfer from perception training to speaking is Low because it was reached only secondhand through [7].
- **What doesn't work: about 0.8, answered with a residual.** Learning styles, rereading, cramming, age, sleep, and first-language glosses are covered. A beginner-specific advantage for first-language glosses is Low. App efficacy has an evidence gap by nature. Subliminal learning was not researched.
- **Motivation, habit, and time: about 0.55, answered with a known residual.** The published hour estimates and the motivation meta-analysis are solid. Whether more total practice time produces more learning is Low because the sources conflict. Self-efficacy was not covered, session length has no direct evidence, and habit formation rests on one general behavior study.

## 4. Recommended Next Steps

- [ ] Schedule delayed recall of each item across separate days, with equal or gently expanding intervals, and count a same-session repeat as practice, not as the review [1][2].
- [ ] Make every review an active recall with immediate feedback. Show the answer only after an attempt [1][12].
- [ ] Offer both recall directions per item, and weight production recall (from meaning to target word) when the learner's goal is speaking or writing [15][16].
- [ ] Include typed or spoken recall alongside multiple choice, and treat the order of formats (recognition first, then recall) as a design choice to test, because the evidence conflicts [15][17][18].
- [ ] Track how many times and in how many different contexts each word has been met, and recycle words into later readings and dialogues [19].
- [ ] Mix previously introduced patterns and item types within review sets instead of practicing one type at a time [21].
- [ ] Grade readings and listening passages by estimated known-word coverage, and prefer texts at or above about 95% known words, moving toward 98% [3]. Treat the listening target as provisional, because it is extrapolated from reading.
- [ ] Add tap-to-gloss on unknown words, with first-language glosses by default and an optional multiple-choice gloss mode [23][24].
- [ ] Let the learner control audio pace and make synchronized text optional, not mandatory [25].
- [ ] Present each grammar pattern as a short explanation followed by meaningful practice, and do not commit the program to only explicit or only implicit teaching [4][5].
- [ ] On an error, prompt the learner to retry before revealing the correct form [6].
- [ ] Add perception drills for hard sounds with immediate right-or-wrong feedback [7][8], using several voices where available, although their added benefit is unsettled [7][28]. Consider following the drills with speaking practice on the same sounds; the evidence that perception gains carry over to speech is Low and secondhand [7].
- [ ] Add pronunciation practice with feedback that continues over weeks, not a one-off module [9].
- [ ] Tie the daily session to a fixed cue, and design streaks so that a single missed day does not reset progress [10].
- [ ] Log total study time and show progress against realistic hour ranges, with a clear note that the published estimates come from full-time instructed training [38].
- [ ] Present new material in more than one modality for every learner, and do not add a learning-style quiz [13][30][31].
- [ ] Do not build features that depend on sleep audio or subliminal exposure [36][37].
- [ ] **Recommend `create-initiative` initiative:** `Yes` — the Do and Don't findings translate directly into program features (spaced recall scheduling, recall direction and format, coverage-graded input, glosses, feedback prompts, perception drills, and habit design) that an initiative can scope and test.

**Disconfirmation notes (one deliberate search per major claim):**

- **Spacing and retrieval.** The search looked for second-language evidence that spacing does not help. It found a second-language meta-analysis that confirms spacing [2], and it overturned one common design belief, because expanding schedules were not superior [1][2].
- **Recall versus recognition (remediation loop 1).** The search looked for meta-analytic evidence that recognition practice matches or beats recall. It found it, as reported in [18], so the format finding is held at Low.
- **Explicit grammar.** The search looked for meta-analytic evidence that implicit instruction matches or beats explicit instruction. It found a later meta-analysis reporting only a minor difference overall [5], so the explicit-versus-implicit finding is held at Low.
- **Input and audio support.** The search looked for evidence that reading while listening is better. It found a trivial overall effect that disappears when learners control the pace [25].
- **Corrective feedback type.** Two meta-analyses disagree on whether explicit or implicit feedback lasts better [6][26], so feedback type is held at Low.
- **Perception training.** The search looked for limits. It found that the multi-talker advantage on immediate outcomes disappears after outlier removal and publication-bias correction [28].
