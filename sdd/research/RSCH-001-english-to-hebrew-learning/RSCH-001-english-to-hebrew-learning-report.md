## 1. Findings

**Recommendation:** For an English-speaking adult, a faster self-study session mixes comprehensible Modern Hebrew, delayed recall of the same items, one simple explicit pattern, pronunciation practiced in a real phrase, and then speech aimed at the message. No published trial shows that any one Hebrew method is faster for this group. **Moderate**

- No published comparison shows that a script, sound, or morphology drill speeds acquisition for English-speaking adults learning Modern Hebrew. A 2017 review of Hebrew as a second language says early vowel pointing deserves attention and leaves a pointed-then-unpointed sequence untested [1]. What does exist is processing evidence, not a speed trial: proficient English-speaking readers of unpointed Hebrew already decompose roots and patterns much as native readers do [2], while advanced late learners (Spanish- or Portuguese-speaking, not English-speaking) were less sensitive to verb-pattern class than native speakers [3]. **Low: absence of a method trial, and absence claims stay at Low. Hebrew-specific.**
- When Hebrew trials are thin, spacing and retrieval are strong enough to borrow. They are general, not Hebrew-specific. Spacing retrieval attempts beat massing them in a general memory meta-analysis (g = 0.74); expanding schedules were not superior [6]. Practice tests beat restudy in a broad education meta-analysis [7]. A 2026 restatement of the four strands puts about three-quarters of time on meaning-focused input, output, and fluency, and about one quarter on deliberate study that includes spaced retrieval; the equal split is informed judgment, not a measured optimum [8]. A second-language spacing meta-analysis was not used, because its public page could not be read on the last check. **Moderate: two independent reviews that could be read agree that spacing and testing beat massed restudy, and a third source places spaced retrieval inside deliberate study. General second-language research applied to Hebrew.**
- For adults, an input-only or grammar-last rule is too strong. A 2000 meta-analysis found that explicit instruction outperformed implicit instruction [9]. Output and interaction are treated as causes of development, including noticing, in a 2018 review [11]. A pedagogical article on Israeli Hebrew argues that one-step transparent rules may be stated directly and that complex patterns should be induced from input; the text was read through an intermediary copy and is not a trial [12]. **Moderate for the general explicit-instruction claim: one high-quality meta-analysis. Low for the one-step Hebrew rule: one pedagogical text, not a trial. General second-language research, with one Hebrew application.**
- A list of English speakers’ Hebrew production errors, and a training study that prevents them, was not found. In other languages, contextualized pronunciation practice is what has been tied to gains beyond drills [16]. Synthetic speech helped controlled perception and production of one English pattern, and not spontaneous production; the target language was English, not Hebrew [19]. **Low: absence of an English-speaker error list, one pronunciation synthesis, and one English synthetic-speech study.**

Practice order, as an application of those sources and not as a new experiment: on a new item, show the vowel points, because the consonants underdetermine the word [1]. Recall the item from memory after a delay, with feedback [6][7]. Keep deliberate study to roughly a quarter of the session [8], and state only a one-step pattern in that slice [12]. Then use the phrase for a message, including the sounds, in context [11][16]. Fading the vowel points once the word is known is untested [1].

## 4. Recommended Next Steps

- [ ] Build a study session as mostly meaning: Hebrew the learner can mostly understand, the same items recalled later, then a spoken use of the phrase.
- [ ] Keep about a quarter of the session for deliberate work: spaced recall, pronunciation inside a real phrase, and at most one transparent pattern.
- [ ] Show vowel points on a new word. Treat taking them away later as untested.
- [ ] Use a human model for Hebrew sounds. The only synthetic-speech result found was for English, and only for controlled speech [19].
