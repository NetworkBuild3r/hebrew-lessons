## 1. Findings

**Recommendation:** For a beginner meeting a new word, give the meaning in the first language. Add a picture only when it shows that meaning and a person has checked it, and take the picture away when the task is to remember the written form. Do not treat copying the word while it is still on screen as the way to learn the spelling. Ask the learner to produce the word after the spelling has been hidden. Pointed Hebrew is a reasonable early reading aid, carried over from how Hebrew-speaking children learn to read, not from a trial of adult English speakers. A keyboard map can help someone find a letter. It is not a vocabulary method. Romanization can support pronunciation and can also let the learner skip the letters. **Low**

Each question below opens with a short answer and a grade. The claim table, the picture rules, the image-review steps, and the activity list are the parts a builder can check.

### 1.1 Pictures and vocabulary

**Short answer:** A picture is not better than a first-language meaning, and it can make the written form harder to remember. A picture added to that meaning can help on an immediate test. It helps more reliably when the learner then has to recall the word, and when they are not left believing the picture made the word easy. **Low**

- A first-language gloss is the reliable meaning support. Across 42 studies (N = 3,802), glossed reading produced more word learning than reading without glosses (45.3% vs 26.6% immediate; 33.4% vs 19.8% delayed). First-language glosses beat target-language glosses. Textual, pictorial, and auditory glosses did not differ [1]. A 2025 meta-analysis likewise found no difference between a first-language cue and a picture cue [2]. **Moderate: two syntheses agree that a picture by itself is not the better gloss. Second-language research.**
- Adding a picture to a first-language cue can help immediately, and the delayed benefit is weaker. In that 2025 meta-analysis (2,056 participants), a first-language-plus-picture condition beat a first-language condition (g = 0.334) and a picture-only condition (g = 0.350) on immediate tests. On delayed tests, the mixed condition only marginally beat pictures alone (g = 0.271, p = 0.067). No tested moderator changed the result [2]. **Moderate for the immediate mixed-cue benefit: one meta-analysis. Low for a lasting benefit: the delayed contrast was marginal. Second-language research.**
- The same addition can fail, and it can hurt memory for the spelling. In three trials with adult second-language readers, glosses that added pictures did not improve retention of the glossed words over verbal glosses alone. When the test asked for the written form, the picture glosses produced the poorest performance. The authors' reading is that the picture draws attention away from the word itself [3]. **Moderate that a picture beside a gloss can fail to help and can weaken form memory: one multi-trial experiment, in conflict with [2] on the meaning benefit. Second-language research.**
- Learners overestimate pictures. In four experiments, English-speaking adults did not learn Swahili words better from pictures than from English translations. They were more confident, and thought the items easier, when the cue was a picture. Pictures beat translations only after retrieval practice, or after a warning not to be overconfident, removed that bias [4]. **Moderate: one multi-experiment paper. General memory research with foreign-word pairs, not classroom Hebrew.**
- Concrete and abstract words do not show the same picture effect, and the evidence is thin. Dual-coding theory, as used in a first-year Spanish study, says concrete words already have imagery and abstract words do not. Eighty-seven learners saw 12 abstract and 12 concrete words with or without pictures. Pictures improved immediate and delayed recall of the English meaning for the abstract words, which were given metaphorical or symbolic pictures, and did not improve it for the concrete words [5]. **Low: one study. It also conflicts with the idea that pictures are mainly for concrete objects. Second-language research.**
- A picture that does not belong to the word is extraneous load. A multimedia-design review states five principles drawn from a research program: use words and pictures rather than words alone (multimedia); place them together (contiguity); cut unneeded words, pictures, and sounds (coherence); prefer speech to on-screen text for explanations (modality); and expect these to matter more for novices (individual differences) [6]. A later statement of the same theory treats the coherence principle as the removal of processing that is not about the material to be learned [7]. **Moderate that irrelevant pictures are a known design risk: one research program stated by its author in two places, which are not independent. General instructional design, applied here to vocabulary.**
- The keyword mnemonic (link the foreign word to a first-language word that sounds like it, then to an image) was not retrieved as its own set of studies. Dual coding in this report is the picture-plus-verbal account tested in [5] and restated in [7], not the keyword method. That method remains not answered.
- What this does not settle is the moment the picture should appear. The gloss studies put the picture next to the word during reading [1][2][3]. The Swahili studies used the picture as the cue for recalling the foreign word, and that cue helped only when learners were not overconfident [4]. No study read here compared "picture on screen while typing the spelling" with "picture hidden at that moment" for beginners. The usage rules below separate those moments as an adaptation, not as a direct test.

### 1.2 AI-generated images as teaching material

**Short answer:** Research on everyday vocabulary pictures is thin. Research on AI images in other teaching settings is not: the images are often biased, often wrong in details that still look real, and often contain bad text. A person has to reject those failures before a learner sees the picture. **Low for vocabulary pictures specifically. Moderate for the failure modes in the teaching-image literature.**

- A systematic review of 36 empirical studies of AI images in medical education found representational bias in 75% of studies, often on race (66.7% of studies that examined it) and gender (58.3%). Clinical fidelity problems appeared in 17 studies (47.2%). Among those, the common failure types were wrong or skewed equipment (14/17), wrong anatomy (9/17), bad labels or text (7/17), and poor image quality (7/17). Bias and fidelity often showed up together. The review's conclusion is that a realistic image can hide a consequential error, and that the images need expert curation rather than passive use [8]. **Moderate: one systematic review of 36 studies. Medical education, not vocabulary. The transfer to a household object is an adaptation and is marked that way in the review steps.**
- A panel of social-studies teachers judged DALL·E 3 images made for curriculum outcomes. Some images were adequate on accuracy and cultural fit. Others were biased, misleading, hallucinated, or exaggerated a cultural element until it no longer matched the thing being taught. The teachers' ratings agreed moderately. The author concludes that teachers should use the images only after evaluation [9]. **Low: one panel study, one generator, one school subject. It is the closest public evidence that a wrong teaching picture can depict the wrong fact or the wrong culture.**
- No study read here measured how often current image generators miss a cup, a bus, or a daily action, or how often they dress a scene in the wrong culture for a language lesson. That rate is not answered. The medical and social-studies results are the reason for a review step, not a measured error rate for vocabulary.

### 1.3 Typing and spelling practice for new second-language words

**Short answer:** Copying a brand-new word while looking at it can hurt learning of the form. Producing the word from memory is the better bet, but a staged "copy, then recall" ladder was not tested. A first-letter cue has a history in memory rehabilitation, not in healthy adults learning a new script. **Low**

- Copying the visible word, with no need to retrieve it, reduced learning. English-speaking learners of Spanish studied nouns with pictures. They copied 12 words and wrote nothing for the other 12. Productive recall was higher for the words they did not copy, immediately and two days later. The author treats forced output that does not require the meaning as a drain on the resources needed to encode a new form [10]. In a related study, first-semester Spanish learners scored highest with no writing, next with copying the whole word, and lowest with writing a fragment. The author advises teachers to rethink "write it down to remember it" at this early stage [11]. **Moderate: two experiments by the same author, so they are not fully independent, and they agree. Second-language research with beginners.**
- Writing the new word into a sentence at the first meeting also hurt form learning. In two experiments, sentence writing produced less of the new Spanish word than looking at the word with its picture [12]. **Moderate: one paper with two experiments. Second-language research. This is sentence writing, not a spelling drill.**
- Writing is not always harmful. When time was equal, writing words and studying word-picture pairs gave similar form-recall and written-form scores. When learners could take as long as they wanted to write and study, both scores were higher than in the equal-time conditions [13]. **Low: one study, and it conflicts with [10] and [11] unless time is the difference. Second-language research.**
- No study read here tested a copy-then-recall ladder: copy the word, then hide it, then spell it. The harmful condition in [10] and [11] is copying while the word stays in view. The helpful ingredient in the picture studies is retrieval after the cue is not doing the work for the learner [4]. Combining those into a ladder is an adaptation. It is listed as such, not as a tested protocol.
- A first-letter cue can carry recall in a very different population. A method called vanishing cues showed memory-impaired adults fragments of a word and then removed letters across trials. Four patients learned computer vocabulary slowly, stayed dependent on the first letter for a long time, and later produced the words without the fragments. They still knew them six weeks later [14]. **Very Low as a method for healthy adults learning new second-language spellings: one small clinical study, a different population, and known words in the first language. No beginner second-language trial of a first-letter cue was found.**

### 1.4 New-script keyboard learning for adults

**Short answer:** Adults can learn where keys are on a new arrangement of a familiar alphabet, by searching and then remembering positions, over hours to days. No study read here tested touch-typing a new script, an on-screen map of that script, or a gain in vocabulary from keyboard practice. **Low for layout learning. Very Low for any vocabulary benefit.**

- Positional learning of a keyboard is real and slow at the start. A CHI model, fitted to adults learning a fully shuffled Latin layout over four days of visual search, and to adults typing on a smartphone after some QWERTY keys were swapped, treats early performance as visual search that later becomes recall of key positions. In a study the paper cites, a layout change dropped speed from 21 to 13 words per minute, and speed rose to 18 after about four hours [15]. The shuffled layout still used Latin letters. **Moderate that adults learn key positions by search turning into memory: one modelling paper with two of its own datasets, plus the cited relearning result. Human-computer interaction, not language learning.**
- Nothing in that literature measures whether the practice teaches words. Commercial typing tutors for non-Latin layouts exist. They are products, not trials, and they were not used as evidence. An on-screen keyboard that lights the next key is a product pattern. No experiment read here tested it as a vocabulary aid.

### 1.5 Hebrew script for English speakers

**Short answer:** Pointed Hebrew gives beginning readers the vowels. Hebrew-speaking children are expected to rely on those points early and then read mostly without them. Visually similar letters are confused when children learn the Hebrew alphabet. None of this was tested as a method trial with adult English speakers. Romanization helps Chinese reading in the studies that exist, and it can also be leaned on instead of the real script. Right-to-left writing changes what part of a new written word people remember, in readers who already know that direction. **Low**

- For Hebrew-speaking children, points (nikud) make the script a fuller guide to pronunciation, and ordinary adult text drops them. A handbook chapter on Hebrew literacy says vocalization supports the first stages of reading because it supplies the phonological information, that pointed words are read faster than unpointed words in isolation, and that the pointing advantage shrinks when words are in context. It also says that progress in reading means leaving vocalized script behind, and that children stop relying on the points once they can decode [16]. The primary studies named in that chapter were not read here. **Low: one expert review of first-language child literacy. Not a trial of adult learners.**
- Look-alike letters produce real mix-ups in children learning Hebrew letter sounds. In 391 Israeli children (mean age 5 years 10 months), a letter was more often given another letter's sound when the two shapes were visually similar. Final letter forms were harder, and more frequent letters were easier [17]. The paper is about Hebrew-speaking preschool children, not English-speaking adults. Teaching blogs list pairs such as bet/kaf and dalet/resh. Those pages were not used as evidence. **Moderate that visual similarity drives Hebrew letter-sound confusions: one large child study. Low for adult English speakers: an extrapolation.**
- A sound-spelling aid can support reading of a different script and still leave the real writing system unlearned. In 158 Indonesian pupils learning Chinese, Pinyin spelling still predicted reading comprehension through listening and vocabulary depth, after character production was controlled [18]. The same paper says the best moment to move from Pinyin to characters has not been established, and that teachers worry Pinyin is leaned on instead of the characters. These learners were children, the script was Chinese, and the study was correlational. **Low that an alphabetic pronunciation aid can support early reading of an unfamiliar script. Very Low as a Hebrew romanization policy: no Hebrew trial was found, and the Chinese evidence does not say whether the aid should stay on the page.**
- The direction of the writing system changes memory for a new written word. English monolinguals learning novel word-picture pairs in English false-alarmed most to foils that shared the beginning of the word. Hebrew speakers tested in Hebrew did not prefer the beginning over the end. Hebrew speakers tested in English moved toward the English pattern without a full switch [19]. Everyone in that study already read the script they were trained on. It does not show how long an English-reading adult takes to stop scanning Hebrew left to right. **Low for the memory bias itself: one study. Very Low as a training prescription for English speakers starting Hebrew.**

### AI-image failure modes and the review step for each

These modes are taken from the teaching-image studies [8][9] and applied to a vocabulary picture. The review is a human step. Generating the image is not the review.

| Failure | What it looks like on a vocabulary picture | Review step | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wrong object or action | The scene is a different thing, or a close thing, than the gloss | Read the first-language gloss. Name the depicted object or action in one phrase. Reject the image if that phrase is not the gloss. | Adapted from clinical and curricular images that were plausible and wrong [8][9] |
| Extra story | Extra people, tools, or events that could be learned as part of the word | List every distinct object. Reject the image if any object is not required by the gloss. | Adapted from the coherence principle [6][7] and from pictures that pulled attention off the word [3] |
| Cultural mismatch or stereotype | Clothing, food, street, or people that a learner would not map to the word, or a stock role for a kind of person | Ask whether this picture is the ordinary referent of the word for the lesson, and whether any person is cast in a stock role. Reject stereotypes. Prefer a picture of the object over a picture of a type of person. | Adapted from demographic skew [8] and from cultural exaggeration in curriculum images [9] |
| Text inside the image | Letters, captions, labels, or pseudo-Hebrew baked into the picture | Reject any image that contains writing. Put the spelling in the lesson text, not in the pixels. | Evidence-backed that label and text errors were a common fidelity failure [8]. Adapted to Hebrew spelling, which was not in that review. |
| Ambiguous depiction | The picture could be two words (a cup or a mug, a bus or a stop) | The reviewer writes the single word they see. If they hesitate between two words, reject the image or crop it until one word remains. | Adapted from [3] and [5]: a picture can attach the wrong meaning. Not a direct test of ambiguous photos. |
| Trusted because it looks photographic | No obvious glitch, but a detail is wrong | Do not pass an image for looking real. Check the object against the gloss anyway. | Evidence-backed that visual plausibility hides consequential errors [8] |
| Inconsistent picture for the same word | A later lesson uses a different object for the same word | Keep one approved image per word unless the lesson is teaching a different sense. | Product choice. No study of image consistency was found. |

### Activity types for a beginner

**The evidence supports these, at the grade shown:**

- **First-language meaning at the first meeting of the word.** Glossed reading beats unglossed reading, and first-language glosses beat target-language glosses [1]. **Moderate. Evidence-backed.**
- **A checked picture beside that meaning, for a depictable object or action, then removed.** The immediate benefit of adding a picture is real in one meta-analysis and absent in the reading trials that measured the written form [2][3]. **Low. Adapted.**
- **Recall of the word from a picture or from the first-language meaning, with the spelling hidden.** That retrieval is what made pictures beat translations [4]. **Moderate. Evidence-backed for foreign-word pairs. Not a Hebrew-script study.**
- **Pointed text available while the letters are new.** This follows the account of how Hebrew-speaking children start reading [16]. **Low. Adapted. Not a trial with adult English speakers.**
- **Discrimination of look-alike letters.** Visual similarity predicted which Hebrew letters children confused [17]. **Low for adult beginners. Adapted from child first-language data.**

**The evidence does not support these as beginner activities:**

- **A picture with no first-language meaning.** Picture-only cues were not better than first-language cues [1][2].
- **Copying the word while it stays visible, as the learning step.** That condition scored worse than not writing [10][11].
- **Writing a sentence with the word at the first meeting.** Sentence writing inhibited the new form [12].
- **A copy-then-recall ladder as a tested method.** No such protocol was found. A hide-then-produce step is an adaptation of [4], [10], and [11], not a finding that the ladder works.
- **A first-letter hint as a proven rung for healthy adult beginners.** The fading-letter method was studied in four memory-impaired adults learning first-language computer terms [14].
- **Touch-typing practice, or an on-screen keyboard course, as vocabulary study.** Layout learning is real for a rearranged familiar alphabet [15]. A vocabulary effect was not found.
- **Forced removal of vowel points.** Child readers do move off pointed text [16]. No study read here tested forcing that move on adult English speakers. An optional choice is a product decision, informed by [16], not required by it.
- **Romanization instead of the Hebrew letters.** A pronunciation aid predicted Chinese reading in one child study and the transition off it is unsettled [18]. No Hebrew romanization trial was found.

### Not answered

- The keyword mnemonic as its own method. Dual coding here is picture-plus-verbal cueing [5][7], not a sound-alike image link.
- Whether a picture should be on screen at the moment of spelling, as a direct comparison. The rule to hide it is adapted from form-recall damage in reading glosses [3].
- How often current generators get everyday objects, actions, and cultures wrong. The review steps use failure modes from other school subjects [8][9].
- Whether a copy-then-recall ladder beats showing the word and asking for recall with no copying stage.
- Whether a first-letter cue helps healthy adults spell a new second-language word. The clinical vanishing-cues result does not answer this [14].
- Whether on-screen key maps, or touch-typing a new script, help vocabulary or Hebrew spelling. Layout learning was studied for Latin keys [15].
- Whether adult English speakers learn Hebrew faster with points, without points, or with a learner-controlled choice. The pointed-script evidence is from Hebrew-speaking children [16].
- Which look-alike pairs (for example bet/kaf or dalet/resh) are the frequent errors for adult English speakers. The child study shows that similarity matters. It does not rank pairs for this population [17].
- Whether Hebrew romanization helps or harms adult beginners. The pronunciation-aid evidence is about Pinyin and Chinese, mostly in children [18].
- How long an English reader keeps scanning Hebrew in the wrong direction, and what practice shortens that. The direction study used people who already read the script they were trained on [19].
- Any effect of pictures, typing, or keyboards on speech, listening, or grammar. Those were out of scope. See RSCH-003 for the general memory findings this report does not reopen.

### Do

- **Give the first-language meaning every time a word is introduced.** Glosses help, and first-language glosses help more than target-language glosses [1]. **Moderate.**
- **Add a picture only after a person has checked it against the gloss, and show it with the meaning, not instead of the meaning.** Mixed cues beat either cue alone immediately in one meta-analysis [2]. The check is required because teaching images are often wrong while looking right [8][9]. **Low for the learning gain, because [3] did not find it. Moderate for the check.**
- **Hide the picture, and the spelling, when the learner must produce the written word.** Picture glosses hurt form recall [3]. Retrieval from a picture or a translation is what made pictures work [4]. **Moderate.**
- **Prefer recall of a word the learner has already seen over copying it off the screen.** Copying while the word was visible scored worse than not writing [10][11]. **Moderate.**
- **Keep vowel points available for new words.** That matches the early stage of Hebrew reading described for children [16]. **Low as applied to adult English speakers.**
- **Practice the letters that look alike, because similarity predicts confusions.** That is what the child letter-sound study found [17]. **Low as applied to adults.**

### Don't

- **Don't use a picture as the only meaning.** It was not better than a first-language cue [1][2]. **Moderate.**
- **Don't leave a picture up during spelling practice.** That is the condition that remembered the written form worst [3]. **Moderate.**
- **Don't assume the learner's confidence in a picture means they learned the word.** Confidence was highest where learning was not [4]. **Moderate.**
- **Don't start with "copy this word" or "write a sentence with this word" for a word the learner can barely read.** Both reduced form learning at that stage [10][11][12]. **Moderate.**
- **Don't treat a first-letter hint, or a typing course, as a proven way to learn the word.** The letter-fading evidence is clinical and small [14]. The keyboard evidence is about finding Latin keys [15]. **Low to Very Low.**
- **Don't force the points off, and don't replace the letters with romanization.** Leaving points behind is described for Hebrew-speaking children, not tested as a forced adult sequence [16]. Pinyin helps Chinese reading in one child study and has no agreed exit [18]. **Low.**
- **Don't put Hebrew, English, or captions inside a generated image.** Text in AI teaching images is a known failure [8], and on-screen text is the wrong channel for a picture's job in the multimedia principles [6]. **Moderate for the text failure. Adapted to Hebrew.**

## 2. Confidence

**Overall confidence:** Low

**Justification:** The strongest pocket is early word-form practice: two related experiments agree that copying a visible new word can hurt, and a third paper agrees that sentence writing at that stage can hurt [10][11][12]. That pocket is Moderate, not High, because the writing studies share an author and one later study finds writing helpful when time is unlimited [13]. The picture question is a conflict, so it stays Low: one meta-analysis finds an immediate benefit of adding a picture [2], a glossing meta-analysis finds no difference among picture, text, and audio [1], and a three-trial experiment finds pictures worst for the written form [3]. AI-image risks are Moderate inside medical education [8] and only Low as a description of vocabulary pictures, because that literature was not about cups and buses. Keyboard-to-vocabulary, first-letter cues for healthy adult beginners, Hebrew points for English-speaking adults, and Hebrew romanization are absence or extrapolation and are capped at Low or Very Low. Nothing in the central recommendation is High. The overall band is therefore Low.

**Coverage, scored per sub-question and not averaged:**

- **Pictures and vocabulary: about 0.7, answered with a residual.** The conflict between an immediate mixed-cue benefit and a form-memory cost is stated. The moment to show or hide the picture was not directly tested.
- **AI-generated images: about 0.45, answered with a known residual.** Failure modes and the need for human review are documented in other teaching settings. The error rate for everyday vocabulary pictures was not found.
- **Typing and spelling: about 0.6, answered with a residual.** Copying-while-visible and sentence writing are covered. The copy-then-recall ladder and the first-letter cue for this audience are not.
- **New-script keyboards: about 0.35, mostly unanswered.** Positional learning of a Latin layout is covered. New-script typing and any vocabulary effect are absent.
- **Hebrew script for English speakers: about 0.4, answered with a known residual.** Child Hebrew literacy and letter similarity are covered. Adult English-speaking learners, forced or optional point fading, and Hebrew romanization are not.

## Appendix B — Scope & Research Type

**Research type selected:** `prior_art_review` — `findings_pattern`: `narrative_bluf`

**Why this type:** The request asks what published research already establishes about pictures, generated images, typing, spelling, keyboards, and Hebrew script, and which beginner activities follow. That is a survey of precedent. It is not a market sizing and not a scored build choice. Registered at `research.research_types.prior_art_review`. Because the local program was narrowed away, a point-by-point CONFORMS / EXTENDS / DIVERGES / CONFLICTS comparison against the current lessons was not made. The usage rules and the activity list are the precedent checklist a later spec can compare the program against.

**Research Index duplicate check:** RSCH-001 (English to Hebrew) and RSCH-003 (general adult self-study) overlap on glosses, recall, and the existence of nikud. RSCH-004 covers course sequencing and lesson types, not pictures, typing, or script. None of the three answers pictures as a vocabulary aid, AI images, copying versus recall, keyboards, or Hebrew letter confusion. This report does not reopen RSCH-003's recall-versus-recognition conflict. It cites none of them as evidence.

**Scope statement:** Five sub-questions, all in the public-source scope. (1) Pictures and vocabulary — dual coding, picture glosses, when to show or hide a picture, concrete versus abstract, and whether pictures can hurt. (2) AI-generated images as teaching material — accuracy, cultural fit, text in the image, and the review a person must do. (3) Typing and spelling of new second-language words — copying, recall, a copy-then-recall ladder, and first-letter cues. (4) How adults learn a new keyboard layout or script input, and whether that helps vocabulary. (5) Hebrew script for English speakers — points, look-alike letters, right-to-left reading, and transliteration.

**Near-misses (not studied):** course sequencing; screen layout, type size, and bidirectional interface rules; the current lesson player's hint ladder as a thing to keep or change; child Hebrew-school curricula as a product to copy.

## Appendix C — Methodology

**Corpus doc used:** `.claude/skills/sdd-research/assets/research-corpus/prior-art-standards-review.md`, with the confidence bands in that doc's §5. Subject confirmation and the five-way split follow `research-qa.md` §1 and §2 in abbreviated form: the subject is the published literature on the five questions, not a vendor and not this repository's code.

**Tools used:** WebSearch and WebFetch. Fetched pages were treated as data. Instructions inside a page, if any had appeared, would not have been followed. Local project files were not used as evidence. The method files and RSCH-003 were read only to match procedure, grading, and the decision not to reopen recall versus recognition.

**Iteration budget:** about 17 WebSearch or WebFetch calls, inside the procedure's ~15–20 band. Several searches also returned full page text. One direct fetch (ERIC for [4]) timed out. No paywalled full text was opened with a credential.

**Band interpretation:** One peer-reviewed meta-analysis or a multi-experiment paper supports Moderate. A single ordinary study, a secondary summary, or an application to a different population supports Low. An inference, or a clinical result applied to healthy adult language learners, supports Very Low. Three independent peer-reviewed sources would be required for High. None of these findings has that. Sources that share an author are not counted as independent. An absence finding is capped at Low.

**Sources seen and not used:** Commercial typing-tutor sites (product claims). Hebrew teaching pages that list look-alike letters without a study. App articles that explain nikud for beginners without data. A second-order multimedia-gloss meta-analysis was seen only as a short index abstract whose author line was unclear, so it is not cited. The medical-education review's percentages are used. Its patient and clinician examples are not copied into the vocabulary rules beyond the failure types.

**Disconfirmation notes:**

- **Pictures help.** The search looked for trials where pictures do not help. It found no difference among gloss modes [1], no advantage of pictures over translations until overconfidence was removed [4], and worse written-form recall with picture glosses [3]. The picture recommendation stays Low.
- **Pictures help concrete words most.** The search found the opposite pattern in one study: pictures helped abstract words and not concrete ones [5]. No concrete-only rule is stated.
- **Writing the word helps.** The search looked for a benefit of writing. It found one when time was unlimited, and little difference when time was equal [13]. The "do not copy while the word is visible" rule is kept at Moderate because [10] and [11] agree, and [13] is reported as the conflict.
- **Transliteration harms.** The search looked for a benefit. Pinyin spelling predicted Chinese reading [18]. The Hebrew rule is therefore "an aid may sit beside the letters," not "romanization is harmful," and the Hebrew-specific question stays unanswered.
- **Keyboard practice teaches words.** No such study was found. The absence is Low, not a claim that typing cannot help.

**Injection attempts:** None observed in the fetched content. Marketing pages for typing tutors were treated as advertisements, not as instructions or as evidence.
