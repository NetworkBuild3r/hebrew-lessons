## 1. Findings

**Recommendation:** Build the course as a fixed spine of frequency band plus theme, then hang a small set of lesson types on that spine. Teach more frequent words before rarer ones, and group a lesson by a situation rather than by a list of words from the same category [1][2][4][5]. Across a unit, give most of the time to meaning (a graded story, listening, a short dialogue, and a fluency pass on already known language) and keep deliberate study of new words and one pattern to about a quarter of the unit [1]. Bring every new word back for recall on a later day. That spacing result is already High in RSCH-003 and is not re-argued here. There is no evidenced count of new words per lesson, no Hebrew sequencing trial, and no trial in which a lesson drafted from a learner's known words beat a fixed frequency-and-theme order. Those three gaps are product choices or open questions, not findings. **Moderate for the shape of the course. Low for every numeric cap a validator would enforce.**

RSCH-001 and RSCH-003 are cross-references. They are not evidence for a new claim. RSCH-003 already rates spacing over cramming as High, and reading coverage near 95–98% known words as Low. RSCH-001 already rates a pointed-then-unpointed Hebrew sequence as untested.

### 1.1 Sequencing across lessons

- The most frequent words do most of the work of covering a text, so a beginner course has a reason to teach them first. Nation's corpus study, read here in its abstract, calculates that if 98% coverage is the goal, written English needs about 8,000–9,000 word families and spoken English about 6,000–7,000 [3]. Schmitt and Schmitt restate Nation's band table: the first 1,000 families cover about 78–81% of written text and 81–84% of spoken text, and each later thousand adds much less [2]. They argue, on that cost-benefit ground, that the first 3,000 English families are the high-frequency band worth teaching directly [2]. The 98% premise itself stays Low, as already graded in RSCH-003. **Moderate that frequency bands are very unequal in text coverage: one corpus study [3], with the band table read in a review that cites it [2]. Low that learners acquire words in frequency order: Nation states it as an assumption, and no acquisition trial was read. English corpus research.**
- A theme is a better way to group the words inside a band than a same-category list. Nation's course sketch says high-frequency items should be learned before lower-frequency ones, and that items learned together should not interfere [4]. He cites Tinkham for the claim that words tied to one situation (a theme) are easier than words from one semantic category, such as a list of body parts [4][5]. Tinkham's abstract reports that semantic clusters hindered learning and thematic clusters helped, relative to unrelated words [5]. **Low: one researcher's experiments, seen here as an abstract, plus one pedagogic paper that applies them. No second independent lab was read. Second-language vocabulary research.**
- No trial was found that ranks frequency order, theme, communicative function, and grammar order against each other for adult beginners. Communicative-function syllabuses were not retrieved as experiments. Grammar order has a constraint, not a syllabus: Pienemann argues that instruction is limited by what the learner is ready to process, and he states that the form of teaching does not follow from that research [6]. **Low for a readiness constraint on some structures: one theoretical article whose opening and hypothesis were read, not a course trial. Very Low as a sequence of grammar lessons. Second-language research.**
- Nothing in this session is a Hebrew sequencing trial, or a trial for another non-Latin script that ranks those four orders. RSCH-001 already records the absence of a Hebrew method trial. **Low: an absence.**

### 1.2 New material per lesson

- There is no evidence-based number of new words, or new patterns, per lesson. The figures that exist are rates per hour, and they describe different activities. Nation reports that about 56 minutes of graded reading produced four words learned reasonably well and twelve partially learned, while deliberate study of word pairs has been reported at about 35 words an hour [1]. Those deliberate-study rates are cited by Nation from older work that was not read here. In a separate course sketch he suggests a planning goal of about 50 words a week on word cards, about two hours of work, and he notes that this is about twice the rate he attributes to European classroom courses [4]. Schmitt and Schmitt quote Milton's survey average of about four words per classroom-contact hour, and they leave open whether that average is a mental limit or a result of courses that do not focus on vocabulary [2]. **Low for each rate: one synthesis or a quotation of a book that was not read. The rates conflict because they measure different work. Second-language pedagogy.**
- A validator still needs a cap, or lessons will grow without a bound. Eight new words in one mixed lesson is a product choice. It is not Milton's four, and it is not Nation's 35. It sits between a short lesson and an hour of pure card study, and it can be counted. **Product choice.**

### 1.3 Recycling earlier words

- Cross-lesson recycling is the spacing result already graded High in RSCH-003 §1.1: recall on later days beats massing the same practice in one sitting. This report does not re-grade that finding.
- How many encounters, and in how many contexts, remains what RSCH-003 already said. More encounters are associated with more incidental learning, and no count guarantees that a word is known (RSCH-003 §1.1, Moderate for the association, Low for the missing threshold). Nation adds a course-level condition: language that is studied deliberately should also appear in the meaning-focused strands, and the most useful items should be met again and again [1]. A fluency activity, in his definition, contains no new language [1]. Generative use (a new context, not the original sentence) is asserted in his course sketch via studies not read here [4]. **Moderate for "bring useful items back in later meaning-focused work," as an application of RSCH-003 plus Nation's course conditions. Low for any required number of encounters or contexts.**

### 1.4 Lesson structure and types

- Nation sorts course activities into four strands: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning, and fluency development [1]. Input and output need mostly familiar language (he cites about 95–98% known running words for vocabulary). Fluency needs familiar language, a focus on the message, pressure to go faster, and a large amount of use. Language-focused work is deliberate study, and he says it should also receive spaced repetition and should show up again in the other strands [1]. **Moderate that these four opportunity types are a usable checklist: one widely cited framework whose conditions were read. The conditions match RSCH-003 on coverage, spacing, and deliberate study. They are not a new experiment.**
- An equal share of time for each strand is Nation's proposal, and he calls that equal split arbitrary [1]. RSCH-001 already records the same point from his 2026 restatement. Three of the four strands are about meaning, so the proposal is also "about three-quarters meaning, about one-quarter deliberate study" [1]. **Low for any exact percentage. The quarter is a planning rule he adopts, not a measured optimum.**
- A unit can hold all four strands on one theme: deliberate study, then input or output, then fluency on the same material [1]. His course sketch names the activity families that fill those strands: graded reading and listening to stories (input), speaking and writing on the same content (output), word-card study and a short focus on form (language-focused), and a fluency pass with no new items, such as 4/3/2 or easy reading [4]. The 4/3/2 accuracy and speed gains are studies he cites that were not read here. **Low for those named activities as a set: one author's course sketch. Moderate only for the strand labels themselves, above.**
- Task-based programs are not a settled replacement for that mix. Bryfonski and McKay's abstract reports a large effect for task-based programs (d = 0.93) across 52 studies [8]. Boers and Faez, read here, say the comparative studies behind that figure mostly do not support a meta-analysis: many outcomes were confounded, and many so-called tasks were language exercises. They report that a later reanalysis of a subset (Xuan and colleagues, 2022) found a smaller effect (g = 0.61) and still judge the field not ready for a relative-effectiveness meta-analysis [9]. **Low: the published estimates conflict, and the critique was read. Second-language program research.**
- Listening coverage is not the reading figure copied across. For four short informal narratives, 40 learners of English scored a mean of 7.65 out of 10 at 95% coverage and 7.35 at 90%, with no significant difference between those two, and with a wider spread of scores at 90% (range 3–10 versus 5–10) [7]. Comprehension at 98% and 100% was significantly higher [7]. This is one study of factual questions on informal stories. It answers RSCH-003's open listening question only for that text type. **Low: one experiment. Second-language listening research.**

### 1.5 Beginner scaffolding

- No study was found on when adult beginners should face comprehension questions, or on whether those questions overwhelm them. Van Zeeland and Schmitt used factual questions as a way to score listening [7]. That shows a test format, not that questions should be part of a lesson. Day and Park's teacher guide on writing questions was seen in search results and was not used. **Not answered. A literal, optional question is a product choice.**
- Script-first versus words-first is not settled for Hebrew. RSCH-001 already states that early vowel pointing deserves attention and that a pointed-then-unpointed sequence is untested. For Chinese, a systematic review of 30 reports found mixed results on teaching characters immediately versus after a pinyin stage, and it judged the methods too weak for a recommendation [13]. Chinese characters are not the Hebrew alphabet, so the transfer is an inference. **Low that no single orthography method has reliable support in Chinese: one systematic review that refuses a winner. Very Low for Hebrew. Absence for a Hebrew script-first trial, which stays Low.**
- Romanization as the form the learner reads is not supported. The same Chinese review reports that pinyin-only work and character work trade off (speech versus recognition) in studies it rates as weak [13]. Nothing read here tests Hebrew transliteration. **Low for Chinese, and only as a mixed, low-quality pattern. Very Low for Hebrew. Whether a Hebrew pronunciation hint helps is not answered.**

### 1.6 Adaptivity and personal sequencing

- Computer tutors that model the learner often beat ordinary group instruction and non-adaptive software. Across 107 effects (14,321 participants), intelligent tutoring systems beat teacher-led large-group instruction (g = 0.42), non-intelligent computer instruction (g = 0.57), and textbooks (g = 0.35), and they did not differ from one-to-one human tutoring [11]. A language-learning meta-analysis of AI-guided individualization found a between-group effect of d = 0.39 against business-as-usual (35 samples, N = 6,020) and a within-group change of d = 1.18 with no comparison group [10]. The between-group figure is the one that answers "better than the usual condition." The within-group figure does not. The language samples are English, and they include literacy tutors and Duolingo-style paths, not a test of Hebrew lesson order [10]. **Moderate that individualized tutors beat typical group or non-adaptive software in the pooled studies: two meta-analyses. The language one is not only adult foreign-language courses.**
- No study was found in which the next lesson, chosen from a learner's known words, beat a fixed frequency-and-theme order. Nation does recommend a vocabulary-size test so the teacher starts in the right frequency band [4]. That is placement into a band, not a personal sequence of lessons, and no outcome trial of that test was read. **Low: an absence for known-word sequencing, and one pedagogic recommendation for a size test.**

### 1.7 Pace and session length

- No study of the best lesson length, or of days per week, for adult self-study was found. One web-app experiment kept total time fixed and compared one 80-minute session, two 40-minute sessions, and four 20-minute sessions of Finnish-German word pairs (N = 79). Four sessions beat one session by 24.7 percentage points of recall [12]. Assignment to schedules was not fully random. Twenty minutes was the size of a block, not a tested optimum against 10 or 30 minutes. The result agrees with RSCH-003's High spacing finding and does not add a session-length rule. RSCH-003 already grades total hours and habit cues; those grades stand. **Low: one applied study that replicates spacing. The best length and the best weekly frequency are not answered.**

## 3. Design rules

A validator can check these. The label is the authority for the rule, not a wish for a stricter rule.

1. **Evidence-backed (Low, reading; already graded in RSCH-003).** A meaning-focused reading counts as input only if about 95% or more of its running words are treated as known. Ninety-eight percent is the stricter target, not a second rule.
2. **Evidence-backed (Low).** A short listening passage aimed at comprehension is not failed for sitting under 98% known words. The working target for that passage is about 95% [7].
3. **Adapted.** A fluency activity contains no new word and no new pattern [1]. The check is the empty new-item list. The learning gain from fluency practice was not re-measured here.
4. **Adapted.** In a unit, language-focused activities are at most about one quarter of the unit's activities [1]. The check is a count of activities by strand, not a claim that 25% is optimal. Nation calls the equal split arbitrary [1].
5. **Evidence-backed (High via RSCH-003, not re-argued).** Every new word has a recall event on a later calendar day, not only inside the lesson that introduced it.
6. **Adapted (from RSCH-003 Moderate encounters, Low for a threshold).** Every new word appears again in a later lesson in a different sentence. The check is that second sentence. There is no required count.
7. **Product choice.** A lesson introduces at most eight new words. No source sets this cap. Section 1.2 is the reason a cap exists at all.
8. **Adapted (Low).** The new words in one lesson are not a single semantic category (colors, body parts, days of the week, near-synonyms) [4][5]. A theme that mixes different kinds of words is allowed.
9. **Adapted (Moderate for unequal coverage [2][3]; Low as a learning-order trial).** New words come from the current frequency band before the next band is opened, except words the theme needs that the story cannot avoid. This check waits on a frequency list. No Hebrew list was validated here (Not answered).
10. **Product choice.** A lesson introduces at most one new pattern, and that pattern also appears in the unit's story or dialogue [1]. Which pattern comes first is not fixed by a trial [6].
11. **Product choice, consistent with RSCH-001.** A new Hebrew word is shown with vowel points. Removing the points later is not a required check.
12. **Product choice.** The learner is not asked to read or produce a romanized form as the correct answer. A pronunciation hint may be shown. Section 1.5 did not test that hint.
13. **Product choice.** Comprehension questions are not required to finish a story. If they are present, the answer is stated in the text, and the question may be in English. They are not the recall event in rule 5.
14. **Adapted (Low).** A personal queue may insert a review that is due, or skip a word the learner already knows. It may not reorder unseen words off the frequency-and-theme spine. Section 1.6 did not find a trial in which that reorder won.

## 5. Confidence

**Overall confidence:** Moderate for the course shape. Low for the numeric rules in §3 that are marked product choice or adapted from a single study.

**Justification:** The shape (frequent before rare, theme rather than a same-category list, four strands with deliberate study in the minority, recall on a later day) rests on sources that were read [1][2][3][4], plus the High spacing result already in RSCH-003. The equal time split is arbitrary on Nation's own page [1]. New words per lesson, comprehension questions, Hebrew script order, transliteration, known-word lesson order, and session length are absences or product choices. Task-based program effects are Low because a large abstract estimate [8] does not survive the critique that was read [9]. Listening coverage is one experiment [7]. The Chinese orthography review does not transfer to Hebrew [13]. Individualized tutors help in pooled studies [10][11] and still do not answer the sequencing question this course has to ask.

**Coverage, scored per question and not averaged:**

- **Sequencing: about 0.55, answered with a residual.** Coverage and the interference warning are in hand. No horse-race of syllabus types, and no Hebrew trial.
- **New material per lesson: about 0.35, not answered as a number.** Hourly rates exist and disagree. The per-lesson cap is a product choice.
- **Recycling: about 0.8, answered by RSCH-003 plus Nation's course conditions.** No new encounter threshold.
- **Lesson structure and types: about 0.7, answered with a residual.** The strand checklist is usable. The time split and the task-based alternative are not settled.
- **Beginner scaffolding: about 0.3, mostly not answered.** Hebrew pointing stays the RSCH-001 open question. Chinese evidence is weak and is the wrong script.
- **Adaptivity: about 0.5, answered with a residual.** Tutors beat ordinary conditions in pooled studies. The known-word spine does not.
- **Pace and session length: about 0.4, not answered as a schedule.** One study repeats the spacing result. Minutes and days per week are open. RSCH-003's hour estimates are not repeated.

## Appendix C — Methodology

**Corpus doc used:** `.claude/skills/sdd-research` prior-art procedure, with the RSCH-003 report as the layout and grading model. The local copy of the corpus file was not required beyond that report's stated bands: three independent peer-reviewed sources for High; one meta-analysis or large primary study for Moderate; a single ordinary study, a secondary summary, or an abstract-only source for Low; an inference for Very Low.

**Tools used:** WebSearch and WebFetch. The Read tool was used on the spec, on RSCH-001 and RSCH-003, on the Research Index, and on the public page text that the web tools stored. No lesson file and no learner record was used as evidence.

**Iteration budget:** 12 web searches, above a tight focused budget and below RSCH-003's hard stop of 30. Several searches returned an open PDF directly, which was then read. Full texts on SAGE and Cambridge that did not include the article body were not used as the source of numbers; the Boers and Faez page did include the article text and was used [9].

**Band interpretation:** Same as RSCH-003. A product choice is an additional label for a rule a validator can check when the evidence does not set the number. It is not a grade of evidence.

**Sources seen and not used:** A teacher blog that assigned words-per-lesson ranges to Nation and Schmitt without matching their pages. Commercial Hebrew-course pages. A Wikiversity Hebrew lesson. The Wikipedia summary of the teachability hypothesis. The Day and Park teacher guide on writing comprehension questions, which is advice rather than a learning trial.

**Disconfirmation notes:**

- **Equal strand time.** Nation's own paper calls the equal split arbitrary [1]. The quarter-time rule stays adapted, not evidence-backed as an optimum.
- **Words per hour.** Schmitt and Schmitt quote four words per classroom hour and immediately ask whether that rate is a limit or an artifact [2]. It is not used as a lesson cap.
- **Task-based programs.** The search for a large positive meta-analysis found one [8] and then a critique that withdraws the basis for that estimate [9]. The grade stays Low.
- **Listening coverage.** The search was for a direct listening study, because RSCH-003 had only a reading extrapolation. One study was found [7]. It lowers the open question from "no study" to "one study of short narratives." It does not raise the grade above Low.
- **Orthography order.** The Chinese review was the disconfirmation for "teach the script before the words" and for "delay the script." It supports neither recommendation [13].
- **Adaptivity.** The search looked for known-word lesson order versus a fixed syllabus. It found tutor-versus-classroom meta-analyses [10][11], which answer a different comparison.

**Injection attempts:** None observed in the fetched pages that were read. A graphical summary on the Chinese review page offered teaching tips; the graded claim uses the review's own conclusion, which refuses a winning method [13].
